Federal

Labor

1 update

The big picture

The one federal labor item this holiday week is narrow but practical: Labor Department guidance on how salaried school staff qualify for family and medical leave. It matters most for school districts and other employers that do not track the hours of salaried workers.

Labor Department: a school specialist's after-hours IEP work counts toward family-leave eligibility

Agency opinion letter (Nov. 17)

What changed
In an opinion letter dated Nov. 17, the Labor Department said a salaried occupational therapist's after-hours work on IEPs (individualized education programs, the legal plans for students with disabilities) must be counted as hours of service under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA). It said the therapist's travel time between schools also likely counts.
What it does
To qualify for FMLA, a worker generally needs 12 months with the employer and at least 1,250 hours of service in the past year. FMLA guarantees up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave a year. The department said that when an employer has not kept accurate hours, it is up to the employer to prove a worker does not qualify, and that for salaried workers exempt from overtime recordkeeping, eligibility is presumed after 12 months on the job.
Who it affects
School districts and their salaried specialists, such as occupational therapists, speech therapists, and special education staff, and more broadly any employer that does not track salaried employees' hours. Opinion letters are guidance, not new regulations.

Health care

1 update

The big picture

Medicare finished its third round of drug price negotiations, and for the first time the list includes drugs given in doctors' offices, not just pharmacy drugs. The new prices start in 2028. Separately, states are deciding whether to use the federal six-month grace period on Medicaid work requirements; Massachusetts opted in this week (see the Massachusetts health care block).

Medicare sets negotiated prices for 13 more drugs, starting in 2028

Final negotiated prices; take effect Jan. 1, 2028

What changed
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) announced negotiated prices for 13 drugs in the third round of the Medicare Drug Price Negotiation Program, created by the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act. This is the first round to include Part B drugs, which are given in a doctor's office, alongside Part D pharmacy drugs.
What it does
CMS estimates the prices would have saved Medicare $9.2 billion, about 45% of net spending, had they been in effect in 2025; counting a separate drugmaker discount program, the savings figure is about $5.5 billion (33%). Fifteen drugs were selected, but no negotiated price applies to Xeljanz and Xolair because cheaper generic or biosimilar versions are now on the market, or to Botox while a court order in a lawsuit by its maker remains in effect. In four cases the drugmaker accepted CMS's written final offer; the rest were agreed in talks.
Who it affects
In 2025 about 1.4 million Part D enrollees and about 120,000 Part B enrollees used the selected drugs, which treat cancer, immune conditions, diabetes, and other chronic illnesses. CMS projects beneficiaries will save about $69 million out of pocket in Part D and $395 million in Part B once the prices apply. With this round, 37 drugs will have negotiated Medicare prices.
What's next
Prices take effect Jan. 1, 2028. The Botox exclusion depends on the outcome of AbbVie v. HHS.

Education

1 update

The big picture

With federal funding set to run out Dec. 11, the Senate Appropriations Committee's plan for the Education Department differs sharply from the House's. The Senate plan would raise the department's budget 19%; the House committee's plan would cut it 10%. Congress must pass a funding bill or another stopgap by the deadline.

Senate committee's 2027 plan would raise Education Department funding 19%; House plan would cut it 10%

Committee proposal; funding deadline Dec. 11

What changed
The Republican-led Senate Appropriations Committee proposed $93.9 billion for the Education Department in fiscal year 2027, a 19% increase over 2026.
What it does
Title I aid for schools in low-income communities ($18.4 billion), English-learner aid ($890 million), and the Office for Civil Rights ($140 million) would stay level. Special education would get $50 million more ($15.5 billion), charter schools $60 million more ($500 million), and after-school programs $5 million more ($1.3 billion). A companion health bill would add $50 million for Head Start ($12.4 billion). The House committee's plan, advanced in June, would cut the department to $71 billion, including a $2 billion cut to Title I; the president's budget asked for $76.5 billion. Subcommittee chair Sen. Shelley Moore Capito (R-W.Va.) said the plan makes meaningful investments; Sen. Patty Murray (D-Wash.) said it does not do enough to stop the department's dismantling or invest in families.
Who it affects
Public schools, especially high-poverty districts and students with disabilities, and Head Start programs. Final numbers depend on a House–Senate agreement.
What's next
Current stopgap funding expires at the end of Dec. 11. Congress must pass full-year bills or another stopgap, or a shutdown begins.

Trade

1 update

The big picture

The administration proposed new tariffs on 14 trading partners under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, the law it has relied on since the Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under an emergency-powers law in February. Nothing takes effect yet: a public comment period comes first, and officials say January is the earliest start.

U.S. proposes new tariffs on China, the EU, and 12 other economies over 'excess capacity'

Proposed; comment period first, earliest effect about January

Source note

Some details here come from an outlet with a weaker reliability rating; the core facts are backed by a highly rated source.

  • Rated mixed reliability. MS NOW (formerly MSNBC) — lean: Left. Media Bias/Fact Check: Left / Mixed factual. Ad Fontes Media: Strong Left (opinion-heavy) / Mixed reliability.
What changed
On Nov. 25 the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) found that 14 economies' industrial policies, such as subsidies that let factories produce far more than demand supports, unreasonably burden U.S. commerce, and proposed tariffs in response. The finding follows an eight-month investigation of 16 economies; probes of Mexico and Norway continue.
What it does
China would face a new 20% tariff on top of the roughly 25% tariffs from Trump's first term, rising to 40% if a U.S.–China trade truce that expires in January breaks down; about $30 billion of Chinese imports under review for tariff cuts would be excluded. Vietnam would also face 20%. For other partners, officials said the new tariffs would replace, not add to, existing ones, and would stay within caps in earlier trade deals, such as 15% for the EU, Japan, and South Korea. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said that 'when it all nets out, it really will just top up a few percentage points.'

U.S. importers and consumers of goods from China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand, and Vietnam. Targeted sectors in the investigation included steel, cars, chips, and solar panels. China's foreign ministry called 'overcapacity' a 'false issue.'
What's next
Exact rates for most countries will be published in the Federal Register, followed by a public comment period.

Presidential memo directs the Justice Department to seek death sentences in more cases and speed executions

Presidential memorandum signed Nov. 23

What changed
A memorandum to the Attorney General directs federal prosecutors, where the law allows, to take federal jurisdiction and seek the death penalty for the first-degree murder of a child and for murders committed during rape, child sexual exploitation, sex trafficking, kidnapping, or human trafficking. This adds to categories named in a January 2025 executive order.
What it does
The Attorney General must work to carry out federal death sentences 'as expeditiously as possible,' including by prioritizing quick resolution of appeals and habeas corpus petitions (court challenges to whether a prisoner is lawfully held), opposing what the memo calls 'dilatory' litigation tactics, seeking expedited court review, and cutting administrative delays. The memo does not change the law; courts still control the pace of appeals.
Who it affects
Defendants in federal capital cases and people on federal death row, along with victims' families. It applies only to federal cases, not state prosecutions.

Executive order requires federal contractors to notify the White House before answering Congress

Executive order signed Nov. 20; published Nov. 25

What changed
An executive order requires all federal contractors and subcontractors to notify the White House and their contracting agency of any pending or future congressional request about their work or communications with the executive branch, and to follow White House instructions on withholding information the administration says is covered by executive privilege (the president's power to keep some internal communications confidential).
What it does
Agencies must inform contractors of the requirement and coordinate responses. The Office of Management and Budget will issue compliance guidance. Contractors that do not comply could face contract termination, suspension, or debarment (a ban on future federal contracts). The order says some congressional leaders have been 'harassing' contractors and that coordination avoids duplicate litigation; its legal limits have not yet been tested in court.
Who it affects
Federal contractors and subcontractors of every size, and congressional committees seeking information from them, including the incoming Democratic majorities that take over in January.

Housing

1 update

The big picture

A new HUD rule would end federal housing aid for 'mixed-status' families, households where at least one member lacks eligible immigration status. Twenty-one states, including Massachusetts, have sued to stop it before it takes effect Dec. 14.

21 states, including Massachusetts, sue to block HUD rule ending housing aid for mixed-status families

Final rule takes effect Dec. 14; lawsuit filed

What changed
The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) finalized a rule that would deny federal housing assistance to a household if any member lacks eligible citizenship or immigration status. Twenty-one states and Pennsylvania's governor sued to block it.
What it does
For more than 30 years, mixed-status families have received prorated aid, reduced to exclude the ineligible member. The rule would end aid for the whole household, require everyone to verify status, and require housing authorities and private landlords to report tenants who fail verification to the Department of Homeland Security. HUD Secretary Scott Turner said the rule enforces a 1980 law and frees up to $218 million for eligible Americans on waitlists; the states argue Congress never gave HUD this power and that U.S.-citizen children could lose their homes.
Who it affects
HUD and DHS identified about 20,000 mixed-status households receiving federal housing aid nationwide. California's attorney general estimated 7,000 households in that state alone.
What's next
The rule takes effect Dec. 14 unless a court blocks it.

Massachusetts is using every available delay on new federal Medicaid work rules, while a state bill to strengthen primary care remains stuck in House–Senate talks. Both stories turn on deadlines: Jan. 1 for the federal rules and Jan. 5 for the Legislature's session.

MassHealth opts into six-month grace period on federal Medicaid work rules

State decision announced Nov. 24; grace period Jan. 1–June 30, 2027

What changed
MassHealth, the state's Medicaid program, said it will use the optional six-month 'hold harmless' period offered by the federal Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Members who would otherwise lose eligibility on Jan. 1, 2027, under the new federal work and education requirements will not be cut off before June 30, 2027.
What it does
During the grace period the state will check whether members meet or are exempt from the requirements, created by the 2025 federal budget law known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, but will not deny or end coverage solely for not meeting them. MassHealth also joined a multi-state request for a 'good faith effort' exemption that would delay the requirements altogether until July 1, 2027. It urged members to start meeting the requirements anyway.
Who it affects
About 310,000 MassHealth members are projected to be subject to the new requirements. The Blue Cross Blue Shield of Massachusetts Foundation has projected the eligibility changes could double the state's uninsured rate.
What's next
Enforcement would begin July 1, 2027, unless the federal exemption request is granted or Congress changes the law.

In conference committee since July 31; session ends Jan. 5

New York enacts five-year ban on AI chatbot toys for children 12 and under

Signed into law Nov. 24 (Chapter 416); takes effect in February

What changed
Gov. Kathy Hochul signed a bill (S.9408A) banning the sale of children's toys with a built-in 'AI companion' for five years. New York is the second state to ban such toys, after California, NBC News reported.
What it does
The law defines an AI companion as a system designed to simulate an ongoing human-like relationship, for example by remembering past conversations, asking unprompted emotional questions, or keeping up personal dialogue. 'Children's toy' follows the federal definition: a product meant for children 12 or younger. The law also orders an interagency study by the Department of State, the Office of Mental Health, the Attorney General, and others. Hochul signed it with an approval memo, which in New York usually means agreed changes will follow.
Who it affects
Toy makers and retailers selling in New York, and families with young children.
What's next
Takes effect in February; the ban expires after five years unless extended.

Governors release bipartisan principles for state and federal AI rules

Policy principles released Nov. 22

What changed
The National Governors Association released principles for AI governance developed by a bipartisan council led by Govs. Wes Moore (D-Md.), Mike Braun (R-Ind.), Josh Stein (D-N.C.), and Spencer Cox (R-Utah). They are meant to make state laws more consistent and to inform the incoming Congress as it writes a federal AI framework.
What it does
The principles say both levels of government should regulate: Washington should focus on national and international risks, while states keep the power to govern AI use within their own responsibilities. They call for policies that promote innovation, protect the public, especially children and consumers, and give workers a voice in how AI is deployed. Moore said governors 'agree that humans must stay in control.'
Who it affects
State governments and legislatures, AI companies operating across many states, and workers and consumers covered by state AI laws. The principles are not binding.
What's next
The new Congress takes office in January.

The big picture

The governor's energy affordability bill, meant to cut ratepayer costs by about $10 billion over a decade, is one of 13 bills still in House–Senate talks. The Senate's energy chair now puts its chances at 50-50.

Energy affordability bill's chances '50-50,' Senate negotiator says

In conference committee; session ends Jan. 5

What changed
Sen. Michael Barrett, the Senate's lead negotiator, said talks 'have not gone particularly well' and gave the bill a '50-50 chance' this session. His House counterpart, Rep. Mark Cusack, said he is optimistic a deal can be reached.
What it does
All versions aim to save ratepayers about $10 billion over 10 years, partly by expanding state energy purchasing and tightening rules for competitive electric suppliers, third-party companies that often charge more than utilities. The House version cuts $1 billion from Mass Save, the efficiency program funded by a charge on utility bills, and would lift the ban on new nuclear plants; the Senate version keeps Mass Save's budget, keeps the nuclear ban, and would end a costly gas-pipeline replacement program by 2030. Eversource wrote that neither bill would improve affordability; Barrett called that letter an attempt to sink the bill, while Cusack said stakeholder input is routine.
Who it affects
Massachusetts electricity and gas customers, whose bills are among the highest in the nation; utilities; and the state's climate targets.
What's next
Deadline to pass legislation this session is Jan. 5, 2027.

Massachusetts

1 update

The big picture

Massachusetts is in federal court over whether its new courthouse law can bind federal immigration agents. The state says ICE is violating the law; the federal government says states cannot direct federal law enforcement.

Massachusetts sues ICE over arrests in state courthouses

Lawsuit filed Nov. 23

What changed
Attorney General Andrea Campbell sued to stop U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) from arresting people in and around state courthouses. The suit says at least 38 such arrests have occurred since the courthouse provision of the state's PROTECT Act took effect Nov. 3.
What it does
The PROTECT Act requires officers to show court officials a judicial warrant (signed by a judge) before making a civil arrest, such as for an immigration violation, at a courthouse. The state argues the Tenth Amendment gives it authority over its own courts and that the arrests derail criminal cases. A Homeland Security spokesperson called courthouse arrests 'common sense' because officers know where a person will be and that the person has passed security screening.
Who it affects
Immigrants with state court dates, including defendants, victims, and witnesses, and the state's criminal courts.

The big picture

One bill did finish this fall: an expansion of the 'Move Over' law, which Gov. Healey says she will sign.

Expanded 'Move Over' law heads to Healey, who says she will sign it

Passed Legislature; governor says she will sign

What changed
The Legislature sent the governor a compromise bill expanding the state's 'Move Over' law, which requires drivers to change lanes or slow down when passing emergency vehicles stopped with flashing lights. Healey said, 'It's a matter of public safety… I'm glad that it's on my desk.'
What it does
The bill adds utility vehicles and disabled vehicles to the list of protected vehicles and raises penalties. Earlier versions required repeat offenders to complete a driver program run by the Registry of Motor Vehicles. WBUR described the bill as bringing 'larger fines and more cars' under the law; the final fine amounts were not available in a readable source at publication time.
Who it affects
All Massachusetts drivers, plus utility crews, tow operators, and stranded motorists on the roadside.
What's next
Takes effect after the governor signs it, on the schedule set in the bill.

New Hampshire

1 update

The big picture

With the new Legislature sworn in next week, some mayors are filing bills to shift costs from local property taxes back to the state, especially for pensions, education, and shared tax revenue.

Mayors file bills to restore state aid to cities and towns

Bills filed for the 2027 session

What changed
Laconia Mayor Mike Bordes, also a newly elected state representative, has filed bills to raise the share of state meals and rooms tax revenue returned to municipalities from 30% to 40% over four years, and to have the state again pay part of local pension costs.
What it does
The state once paid 40% of local contributions to the New Hampshire Retirement System for police, firefighters, and teachers, then cut that share over time to nothing; Bordes's bill would restore it to 17.5%. Somersworth Mayor Matt Gerding said towns need more state education funding and described 'a decade of cuts' pushed down to local property taxpayers.
Who it affects
Property taxpayers and municipal budgets statewide, and local police, fire, and school employees whose pensions cities must fund.
What's next
The new Legislature is sworn in next week; bills get hearings in the new year.

The big picture

A second justice is leaving the New Hampshire Supreme Court this year, which will leave the five-seat court with three sitting justices until the governor fills the seats.

Justice Bryan Gould to retire, leaving two vacancies on the NH Supreme Court

Retirement announced Nov. 23; leaving before year's end

What changed
Associate Justice Bryan Gould announced he will step down for health and family reasons before the end of the year. He is the second justice to leave in two months, after Chief Justice Gordon MacDonald resigned in September following a judicial conduct investigation.
What it does
With no nominee yet for MacDonald's seat, the five-member court will have three sitting justices. In New Hampshire the governor nominates justices and the Executive Council confirms them. The court said Gould will spend his remaining weeks finishing opinions in cases he has heard.
Who it affects
Anyone with a case before the state's highest court, where cases may be decided by a smaller panel until the seats are filled.
