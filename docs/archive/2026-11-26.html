Federal

Labor

1 update

The big picture

The week's main federal labor move changes who counts as an employee. It matters because employee status decides who gets minimum wage, overtime, and family leave.

Labor Department finalizes rule making it easier to classify workers as independent contractors

Final rule; takes effect 60 days after Federal Register publication

Source note

Some details here come from an outlet with a weaker reliability rating; the core facts are backed by a highly rated source.

  • Rated mixed reliability. Washington Examiner — lean: Right. Media Bias/Fact Check: Right / Mixed factual. Ad Fontes Media: Skews Right / Mixed reliability.
What changed
On Nov. 19 the Labor Department finalized a rule that replaces the 2024 standard for deciding whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor. It returns to an "economic reality" test much like the one the department adopted in 2021.
What it does
The test gives the most weight to two questions: how much control the business has over the work, and whether the worker can earn a profit or take a loss through their own initiative or investment. Other factors, such as specialized skill and how permanent the relationship is, count for less. For the first time, the same standard also applies under the Family and Medical Leave Act and the farmworker protection law, not just the Fair Labor Standards Act (the federal minimum-wage and overtime law).
Who it affects
Gig workers, truck drivers, construction trades, and other workers whose status is disputed. Independent contractors are not covered by federal minimum wage, overtime, or family-leave rights. Business groups backed the change, and worker advocates said it will make misclassification easier. The rule does not override stricter state laws such as Massachusetts' worker classification law.
What's next
Takes effect 60 days after it is published in the Federal Register. Legal challenges are likely, as with earlier versions.

Health care

3 updates

The big picture

Three federal moves this week all tighten eligibility and oversight in public health coverage. Medicaid work requirements are getting an optional six-month runway. A new anti-fraud package targets providers and insurance brokers. Stricter ID checks are slowing ACA marketplace sign-ups. Together they shape who keeps coverage in 2027 and how hard it is to enroll.

  1. States get an optional six-month grace period on Medicaid work requirements; four states sue
  2. CMS proposes broad anti-fraud rules for Medicare, Medicaid, and ACA marketplace plans
  3. New ID checks are slowing ACA sign-ups; groups ask CMS to extend the Dec. 15 deadline

States get an optional six-month grace period on Medicaid work requirements; four states sue

Federal guidance issued Nov. 13; lawsuit pending

Source note

Some details here come from an outlet with a weaker reliability rating; the core facts are backed by a highly rated source.

  • Rated low reliability. The Epoch Times — lean: Right. Media Bias/Fact Check: Questionable (Right) / Mixed factual. Ad Fontes Media: Skews Right / Mixed reliability.
What changed
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) said on Nov. 13 that states can take a six-month "hold harmless" period before enforcing the new Medicaid work requirement. The requirement still formally starts in January. Separately, Oregon, Minnesota, New Mexico, and Washington have sued to stop the requirements.
What it does
Under the 2025 budget law, many adults covered through Medicaid expansion must show at least 80 hours a month of work, volunteering, or school to keep coverage. During the grace period, states must still set up the system and check compliance, but people would not lose coverage for failing the requirement until mid-2027. The lawsuit argues that the federal government lacks the authority to impose the changes the way it has and that withholding Medicaid funds would be unconstitutional.
Who it affects
Millions of low-income adults on Medicaid, and the state agencies that must verify their hours. Some states, including Iowa, South Dakota, Indiana, and Nebraska, have said they will not use the grace period. According to The Epoch Times, 19 states and D.C. have asked for a broader delay.
What's next
Requirement begins in January. States choosing the grace period would start enforcing by about July 2027.

CMS proposes broad anti-fraud rules for Medicare, Medicaid, and ACA marketplace plans

Proposed rule; comments due Jan. 19, 2027

What changed
On Nov. 16 the administration proposed a package of anti-fraud rules covering Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, and the Affordable Care Act (ACA) insurance marketplaces.
What it does
State Medicaid agencies could suspend payments to a provider based on a credible fraud finding by CMS or another federal agency. Providers would have to report and return overpayments within 60 days. Insurance agents and brokers with certain felony convictions would be barred from the ACA marketplaces, and CMS could suspend brokers facing credible abuse allegations. CMS estimates about $90 billion in federal savings over 10 years.
Who it affects
Health care providers and insurers billing federal programs, ACA insurance brokers, and state Medicaid agencies. Patients are affected indirectly if providers face suspended payments.
What's next
Public comments are due Jan. 19, 2027, before CMS can finalize the rule.

New ID checks are slowing ACA sign-ups; groups ask CMS to extend the Dec. 15 deadline

Open enrollment underway; extension requested, not granted

What changed
New identity-verification steps on HealthCare.gov are causing online applications to time out, according to reporting by The Texas Tribune. Advocacy groups and insurance brokers have asked CMS to push the Dec. 15 deadline (for coverage starting Jan. 1) to Dec. 31. CMS has not agreed.
What it does
The checks are part of the administration's effort against improper enrollments. CMS says it flagged 315,000 marketplace plans covering about 760,000 people as potentially fraudulent. All were broker-assisted and lacked a verified Social Security or immigration document number. CMS says help is available through HealthCare.gov, its call center, and local enrollment helpers.
Who it affects
People buying their own health insurance through HealthCare.gov states. Massachusetts and New Hampshire residents are affected differently: Massachusetts runs its own marketplace (the Health Connector), while New Hampshire uses HealthCare.gov.
What's next
Dec. 15 is the deadline for Jan. 1 coverage unless CMS extends it.

Education

1 update

The big picture

Early childhood education is the focus this week: the federal government dropped the requirement that Head Start programs raise teacher pay.

Head Start wage and benefit requirements for teachers are rescinded

Final rule published mid-November

What changed
The federal Administration for Children and Families finalized a rule removing the 2024 requirements that Head Start programs raise teacher and staff pay toward public-school levels and provide specified benefits.
What it does
Head Start programs no longer have to meet the pay-parity and benefit mandates. HHS called them an unfunded mandate and estimates programs avoid more than $2 billion in future costs. When the requirements were adopted, the government had estimated that meeting them without new money could cut funded enrollment by more than 100,000 slots. That was the trade-off supporters and critics argued over.
Who it affects
Head Start teachers and staff, who are often paid far less than public-school teachers, and the low-income families of young children whom Head Start serves. Head Start programs in Massachusetts and New Hampshire are affected too.
What's next
Reports differ on the effective date (about 60 days after publication, around mid-January 2027). The Federal Register notice is the authority.

Middle East

1 update

The big picture

Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the route for about a fifth of the world's oil, remains dangerous. Iran says several more tankers hit mines this week, and the United States responded with new sanctions.

Iran says tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz despite U.S. naval escort

Unconfirmed Iranian claims; U.S. sanctions widened

What changed
Iran's Revolutionary Guard said three oil tankers hit naval mines and caught fire on Nov. 23–24 while under U.S. military escort, and that two more hit mines on Nov. 25 after using what it called an unauthorized route. The U.S. military had not confirmed the incidents. The Treasury Department widened sanctions the same week.
What it does
Iran is demanding that ships coordinate their passage with Tehran, using the threat of mines to enforce it, and it has been pressing that demand since the 2026 Hormuz crisis began. The U.S. escorts and sanctions aim to keep the strait open without accepting Iran's control.
Who it affects
Ship crews and tanker operators, and anyone who pays for fuel: disruptions in the strait push up global oil and gasoline prices.

International law

1 update

The big picture

For the first time, the UN's top court has found that a country committed genocide.

World Court rules Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya

International Court of Justice judgment, Nov. 19 (9–6)

Check the source

This story is not confirmed by a highly rated outlet or an official record. The information comes from:

  • Rated mixed reliability. Al Jazeera English — lean: Center-Left. Media Bias/Fact Check: Left-Center / Mixed factual. Ad Fontes Media: Skews Left / Generally reliable.
What changed
The International Court of Justice ruled 9–6 that Myanmar violated the Genocide Convention through its military's campaign against the Rohingya, a Muslim minority, in Rakhine State. It is the first time the court has found a state responsible for genocide.
What it does
The court ordered Myanmar to support the safe and voluntary return of displaced Rohingya, reunite families, and restore property and rights. This is a ruling against the state, not a criminal case against individuals, and the court has no enforcement power of its own. Myanmar's military-backed government called the ruling "unfair."
Who it affects
Roughly a million Rohingya refugees, most living in camps in Bangladesh, plus countries deciding whether to send Rohingya back. The ruling may also shape future genocide cases.

House and Senate negotiators face a deadline on a bill to steer more health spending to primary care

In House–Senate negotiations

What changed
Lawmakers are trying to settle differences between House and Senate versions of a primary care bill before an approaching deadline.
What it does
Both versions would have hospitals and health systems put 15% of their spending toward primary care without raising total health costs. The Senate version sets 2030 as the target year, and the House version sets 2036. The Senate bill also aims at affordability and long wait times for appointments.
Who it affects
Patients who struggle to find or wait to see a primary care doctor, primary care physicians, and hospital systems that would have to shift spending. A union of Mass General Brigham primary care doctors has urged a final deal.
What's next
A compromise bill needs approval from both chambers and the governor's signature.

School funding is the big New Hampshire education issue heading into the January session. Democrats' new majorities want to change how state aid is distributed, while 91 districts are adjusting to property-tax caps their voters just approved.

Democrats with new legislative majorities plan changes to school funding

Proposals for the 2027 session

What changed
After the Nov. 3 election gave Democrats majorities in the Legislature, Democratic lawmakers say they will revisit how the state funds schools.
What it does
Rep. Dave Luneau plans to reintroduce "opportunity grants," which would end uniform per-pupil state aid and instead adjust funding for each district based on factors like poverty and special education needs.
Who it affects
School districts, especially property-poor districts with high needs, and local property taxpayers, since state aid affects how much towns must raise locally. Republican Gov. Kelly Ayotte, re-elected Nov. 3, would have to sign any change.
What's next
Bills will be filed for the session that begins in January.

91 school districts adopted property-tax caps; budgets must now fit them

Approved by local voters Nov. 3

What changed
Under a new state law, voters in 91 of 168 school districts approved caps on school property-tax growth, clearing the required 60% vote.
What it does
In those districts, school boards must now write budgets that fit the cap on how much the local school tax can grow, unless voters approve exceptions the law allows.
Who it affects
Students, teachers, and school staff in those districts, where budget growth will be limited, and property owners, whose school taxes will rise more slowly.
What's next
School boards are drafting budgets now for spring town and district meetings.

Government & regulation

1 update

The big picture

Washington tightened one of the main ways Chinese companies could still reach top-end American AI chips: renting computing power from data centers abroad.

Commerce Department rule restricts overseas data centers from renting top AI chips to Chinese companies

Interim final rule, in effect; issued mid-November

What changed
The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued a rule closing a "remote access" gap in AI chip export controls. Until now, companies based in China could rent the power of advanced chips housed in data centers outside China, even though they couldn't buy the chips directly.
What it does
Shipping advanced AI chips to a data center anywhere in the world now needs a U.S. license if that data center supplies large amounts of computing power to companies headquartered in China and certain other countries. Licenses are reviewed case by case. Data centers inside the United States are not covered.
Who it affects
Chipmakers such as Nvidia, cloud and data-center operators in Southeast Asia and elsewhere (Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil face new license requirements), and Chinese AI developers. CNBC reports that resellers have started dumping Nvidia chips on the market since the rule was announced.
What's next
Because this is an interim final rule, it is already in effect, and Commerce can still revise it after public comment. A related House-passed bill awaits Senate action.
