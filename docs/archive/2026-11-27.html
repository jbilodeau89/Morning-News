Transportation safety

1 update

The big picture

The federal auto-safety agency wants to give carmakers two more years to meet a 2024 rule requiring automatic emergency braking on new cars and light trucks. When the rule was written, the agency estimated it would prevent hundreds of deaths a year. The agency now says the 2029 deadline may be impracticable.

NHTSA proposes two-year delay of automatic emergency braking requirement for new cars

Proposed rule; comments due Jan. 26, 2027

What changed
The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) proposed moving the compliance date for its automatic emergency braking (AEB) standard from Sept. 1, 2029, to Sept. 1, 2031. Small-volume manufacturers would get until Sept. 1, 2032.
What it does
The 2024 standard requires nearly all new passenger vehicles under 10,000 pounds to warn drivers of a likely crash and brake automatically to avoid hitting a vehicle ahead or a pedestrian, including at night and at higher speeds than many current systems handle. NHTSA says its own testing and meetings with industry, some of whose members challenged the rule in court, created 'uncertainty in whether the fleet can comply' on time. It worries vehicles that cannot be certified would be barred from sale. The proposal also adds test procedures for the system's malfunction warning light and clarifies test-speed tolerances, and the agency asks for comment on other possible changes.
Who it affects
Car buyers, automakers, and pedestrians. In 2024 NHTSA estimated the rule would prevent 362 deaths and 24,321 injuries a year once in effect, at an industry cost of about $354 million a year, mostly for software. A delay would push those benefits back by two years.
What's next
Public comments are due Jan. 26, 2027; NHTSA would then issue a final rule.

Immigration & courts

1 update

The big picture

A second federal judge has blocked President Trump's August executive order narrowing who gets citizenship at birth. The order came after the Supreme Court struck down his first order in June.

Second judge blocks Trump's narrower birthright-citizenship order

Preliminary injunction (Nov. 25); class certified

Source note

Some details here come from an outlet with a weaker reliability rating; the core facts are backed by a highly rated source.

  • Rated mixed reliability. Washington Examiner — lean: Right. Media Bias/Fact Check: Right / Mixed factual. Ad Fontes Media: Skews Right / Generally Reliable, Analysis or Other Issues.
What changed
U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper in Washington granted a preliminary injunction and class-action status blocking the August executive order that sought to deny automatic citizenship to some children born in the U.S.
What it does
The order directed agencies not to recognize citizenship for children whose parents work for foreign governments or international organizations without diplomatic immunity, children born through commercial surrogacy, and children with a parent suspected of gang membership or terrorism. Cooper wrote that the president 'cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship' and that the Citizenship Clause 'promises citizenship to practically all children born in the United States.' The White House called Cooper, an Obama appointee, an 'activist judge,' ABC News reported.
Who it affects
Families in the categories the order targeted, including children of employees of foreign governments and international organizations, and families using surrogacy. The injunction covers the certified class while the case continues.
What's next
The administration can appeal to the D.C. Circuit.

Iraq moves to take over Kurdish region's oil exports and border crossings

Federal government decision upheld by Iraq's top court

What changed
Iraq's government said federal authorities will manage border crossings in the semi-autonomous Kurdish region, approve its oil exports through the federal oil ministry, and take over oil fields in four contested provinces: Kirkuk, Diyala, Salahuddin, and Nineveh. Iraq's Federal Supreme Court backed the decision the next day, and federal forces closed two crossings, one with Syria and one with Iran, DW reported.
What it does
Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi said the move targets oil smuggling. The Kurdish regional cabinet called it a violation of the constitution and 'an attempt to return Iraq to a centralized regime.' Rival Kurdish parties held joint mass protests in Irbil and other cities.
Who it affects
Residents of Iraqi Kurdistan, whose government has largely run itself since 1991 and depends on oil revenue, and buyers of Kurdish oil.

Argentina's Congress has written into law a ban on the central bank printing money to cover government spending. Past money-printing helped drive inflation above 200% in 2023.

Argentina bars its central bank from printing money to fund the government

Final passage, Chamber of Deputies 141–0 (99 abstentions)

What changed
Argentina's Chamber of Deputies gave final approval to a law that defines the central bank's 'primary and fundamental mission' as protecting the value of the peso and bars it from issuing money to finance government spending, Al Jazeera reported, citing Reuters and AP.
What it does
The Senate passed the bill earlier but removed President Javier Milei's proposal to require a two-thirds vote of Congress to remove central bank governors. A strike at one of New England's largest grocery wholesalers is in its fourth week with no talks scheduled until mid-December. The dispute is over the company's plan to replace workers' pensions with a 401(k).

Associated Grocers strike enters fourth week; next talks set for Dec. 17

Strike ongoing since Nov. 2

What changed
About 255 Teamsters Local 633 members at Associated Grocers of New England's Pembroke headquarters and warehouse remain on strike, and the company has not agreed to bargain before Dec. 17, the union said.
What it does
The main dispute is the company's demand to replace the workers' pension, which pays a set monthly benefit in retirement, with a 401(k) account, whose value depends on contributions and investment returns. New Hampshire

1 update

The big picture

New research on school cellphone bans bears on how New Hampshire districts enforce the state's 'bell to bell' law. Schools that lock phones in pouches saw test-score gains; the state law does not require pouches.

Study: locking students' phones in pouches raises math and reading scores

New research (working paper); relevant to NH's school cellphone law

What changed
A working paper by researchers at Dartmouth College, Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research, and the testing nonprofit NWEA found that schools requiring students to store phones in lockable pouches saw gains in math and reading scores.
What it does
The researchers analyzed data from 3.3 million students in 4,600 schools from 2021 to 2026. Gains began in the first year and continued for three more, and they were similar across elementary, middle, and high schools and across students' race, gender, and prior achievement. New Hampshire's law bans student phone use during class, between classes, and at lunch, but leaves enforcement to districts; some, including Lebanon, use an 'off-and-away' rule instead of pouches.
Who it affects
New Hampshire students and the school boards deciding how to enforce the state ban. Pouches cost money, which may matter for district budgets.

Government & regulation

1 update

The big picture

State attorneys general from both parties are trying to stop a bankrupt chatbot company from selling millions of users' private conversations, including those of minors, without privacy protections.

42 state attorneys general move to block sale of Character.AI users' chat histories

Objection in federal bankruptcy case

What changed
A bipartisan coalition of 42 state attorneys general is opposing Character.AI's plan, made during its Chapter 11 bankruptcy, to sell the chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to minors, The Verge reported.
What it does
Chapter 11 lets a company reorganize or sell assets under court supervision. The attorneys general say the proposed sale has no requirement that the buyer honor existing privacy policies, no way for users to delete their data, and no limits on what the buyer could do with it, including reselling it. The first bidder was formed on Nov. 13, 2026, and lists no parent company or investors. Disney bought some of Character.AI's assets and hired its CEO earlier.
Who it affects
Character.AI users, especially minors and their parents, whose private conversations with chatbots could be sold. New chatbot laws in New York, Connecticut, California, and Colorado take effect Jan. 1, 2027.
What's next
The bankruptcy court will decide whether to approve the sale.
