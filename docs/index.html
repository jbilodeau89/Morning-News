Federal

HHS proposes rewriting the rules for federal domestic violence grants

The holiday weekend brought one notable federal policy step. The Department of Health and Human Services filed a proposal to rewrite the regulations for the main federal program funding domestic violence shelters and services, including a new process for stripping a state's official domestic violence coalition of its status. It will be published in the Federal Register on Monday. 1

Health & human services

The Administration for Children and Families proposes to replace the remaining rules for the Family Violence Prevention and Services Act (FVPSA) program, which funds emergency shelter and support services for victims of domestic and dating violence and their children, state domestic violence coalitions, and the national domestic violence hotline. The agency already removed six sections of these rules in August as duplicative or unnecessary; this proposal would rewrite the five that remain. 1

Most of the changes delete definitions and requirements that repeat the underlying law, such as the definitions of 'domestic violence' and 'shelter,' and drop terms like 'primary prevention' that the agency says the new rules would no longer use. Confidentiality protections for survivors' identifying information would stay in the statute, with the agency keeping only requirements that go beyond it. The one major addition is a written process to 'de-designate' a State Domestic Violence Coalition, the federally recognized statewide network that receives FVPSA money, if it fails to meet mandatory requirements or cannot carry out its duties. Comments will be due 30 days after Monday's publication. 1

World

Canada's top court upholds Quebec's religious-symbols ban, as the U.S. enforces its Iran blockade at sea

Plus Venezuela's move to lift a ban on opposition candidates and Africa's first trial of an mRNA Ebola vaccine.

The day's most consequential ruling came from Canada, where the Supreme Court largely upheld Quebec's ban on religious symbols for public employees. In the Iran war, the U.S. disabled another ship trying to leave an Iranian port, and Venezuela's interim government said it would scrap a law used to bar opposition figures from office. 1 3 4

Americas

Canada's Supreme Court upheld Quebec's Bill 21, the 2019 law barring public-sector workers such as teachers and police officers from wearing religious symbols on the job, including hijabs, turbans, yarmulkes, and crosses. Quebec passed it using the 'notwithstanding clause,' a provision of Canada's constitution that lets a legislature override certain rights, including freedom of religion. Chief Justice Richard Wagner wrote that the law is rooted in Quebec's 'legitimate and historical commitment' to a secular state. 1 2

The court did strike one part: banning Quebec's elected legislators from wearing religious face coverings violates their democratic rights. It also ruled that courts may still declare whether a law shielded by the notwithstanding clause is consistent with the Charter of Rights. Muslim, Sikh, and Catholic teachers had challenged the law; Prime Minister Mark Carney said his government would study the ruling. 1 2

Venezuela's National Assembly chief, Jorge Rodríguez, said the legislature will repeal a 2024 law that has been used to bar opposition figures who backed sanctions from holding office, Al Jazeera reported. The repeal comes from transition talks between the interim government of Delcy Rodríguez and parts of the opposition. The interim government has promised free elections but set no date. 4

Middle East

U.S. Central Command said an American aircraft fired on the stern of the Palau-flagged cargo ship Pacific Archer in the Gulf of Oman after it left the Iranian port of Bandar Abbas, disabling its propulsion; the crew was unharmed. The U.S. says its blockade of Iranian ports, in place since July, has now disabled six ships and turned back 158. Kuwait summoned Iran's ambassador over the drone strike that killed two Italian technicians on Thursday, Iran denies involvement. 3

Global health

Uganda began Africa's first trial of an mRNA vaccine against the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola, the strain behind Congo's outbreak, which has passed 12,000 confirmed cases. The trial, run by the Uganda Virus Research Institute with Moderna, will enroll 224 volunteers and is the first to test the vaccine in children as young as 1, Al Jazeera reported. A second vaccine, from Oxford and India's Serum Institute, starts testing in Congo on Monday, and the vaccine alliance Gavi has already agreed to buy 1.5 million doses if trials succeed. 5

New Hampshire

Traffic deaths on New Hampshire roads are down 22% this year

New State Police figures show New Hampshire had 85 fatal crashes through Nov. 10, 22% fewer than the 110 in the same period of 2025, and 91 deaths compared with 116, the Concord Monitor reported. Most of the improvement came among the drivers usually considered highest-risk: two drivers under 21 have died this year, down from eight, and nine drivers over 70, down from 20. 1

The state's Office of Highway Safety said no official study has been done and that there is no single reason for the drop. The trend matters for planning because New Hampshire has the second-oldest population in the country by median age, and the number of older drivers is expected to grow. 1

OpenAI and Apple escalate a court fight over AI devices and employees

OpenAI filed counterclaims against Apple in federal court in California, accusing it of stealing trade secrets and blocking OpenAI's access to hardware suppliers, Al Jazeera reported, citing Reuters. Apple sued OpenAI in July, alleging the ChatGPT maker misused Apple trade secrets by hiring its employees; OpenAI denies that. 1

OpenAI's filing says Apple rehired a former employee who spent six months at OpenAI and gained access to plans for a new device and OpenAI's hardware roadmap. It also says Apple encourages employees to work on personal Apple devices and accounts and then keeps access after they leave, which OpenAI says lets Apple 'monitor and ensnare' workers who move to competitors. About 400 Apple employees have moved to OpenAI, the filing says. The case is a fight over which company will control a coming generation of AI-powered personal devices. 1
