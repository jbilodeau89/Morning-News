Federal

A proposed delay for car-safety braking rules, and another court loss for the birthright-citizenship order

A quiet holiday stretch in Washington brought one regulatory rollback proposal and one significant court ruling.

The federal government's work slowed for Thanksgiving, but two developments will matter well beyond the holiday. The auto-safety agency proposed giving carmakers two more years to install automatic emergency braking on new vehicles, and a second federal judge blocked President Trump's narrower attempt to limit who gets citizenship at birth. 1 3

Transportation safety

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) proposed moving the deadline for its automatic emergency braking (AEB) standard from Sept. 1, 2029, to Sept. 1, 2031, with small-volume manufacturers getting until 2032. The 2024 rule requires nearly all new cars and light trucks under 10,000 pounds to warn drivers of an imminent crash and brake on their own to avoid hitting a vehicle ahead or a pedestrian, including at night and at higher speeds than many current systems handle. 1 2

NHTSA says its own testing and meetings with industry, some of whose members challenged the rule in court, left 'uncertainty in whether the fleet can comply' on time, and that vehicles that cannot be certified would be barred from sale. When it wrote the rule, the agency estimated it would prevent 362 deaths and 24,321 injuries a year at an industry cost of about $354 million a year, mostly for software; a delay pushes those benefits back two years. The proposal also adds a test for the system's malfunction warning light. Public comments are due Jan. 26, 2027. 1

Immigration & courts

In Washington, U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper blocked the executive order President Trump signed in August, after the Supreme Court struck down his first birthright-citizenship order in June. The August order told agencies not to recognize citizenship for children whose parents work for foreign governments or international organizations without diplomatic immunity, children born through commercial surrogacy, and children with a parent suspected of gang membership or terrorism. 3 4

Cooper certified a class of affected families and wrote that the president 'cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship' because the Constitution's Citizenship Clause 'promises citizenship to practically all children born in the United States.' The White House called Cooper, an Obama appointee, an 'activist judge,' ABC News reported. The administration can appeal to the D.C. Circuit. 3

World

Gulf attacks, a power struggle over Iraq's oil, and stalled budget talks in Europe

Plus a central-bank law in Argentina, delayed elections in Guinea-Bissau, and a hung result in New Zealand.

The war with Iran kept spreading across the Gulf this week, while governments elsewhere made decisions about money and elections: the European Union failed to agree on its next long-term budget, Argentina locked in limits on its central bank, and Guinea-Bissau's military rulers pushed back a return to civilian rule. 1 3 4 5 6

Middle East

An aerial projectile hit the Jazan oil refinery on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast, killing a Nepali worker, injuring several people, and halting some operations, Saudi Arabia's Energy Ministry said; the Saudi-led coalition blamed Yemen's Iranian-backed Houthis. The refinery can process up to 400,000 barrels of crude a day, and the strike came a day after the Houthis claimed attacks on Ras Tanura, the kingdom's largest refinery, and the ports of Jeddah and Jazan. 1

In Iraq, the central government said federal authorities will run border crossings in the semi-autonomous Kurdish region, approve its oil exports through the federal oil ministry, and take over oil fields in four contested provinces. Iraq's Federal Supreme Court backed the move, federal forces closed two crossings with Syria and Iran, and rival Kurdish parties led mass protests in Irbil, DW reported. Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi says the goal is to stop oil smuggling; the Kurdish regional cabinet called the decision unconstitutional. 2

Europe

EU leaders ended 12 hours of talks in Brussels without agreeing on the bloc's 2028–2034 budget, and some walked out; negotiations resume in December. France and Italy favor higher spending, while Germany leads those urging restraint. A proposed €45 billion loan to Ukraine is opposed by Slovakia, the Czech Republic, and Hungary, and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said unanimous support for Ukraine aid is a condition for approving the wider budget. 3

Americas and Africa

Argentina's Chamber of Deputies voted 141–0, with 99 abstentions, to give final approval to a law that defines the central bank's main mission as protecting the peso's value and bars it from printing money to pay for government spending, a practice that helped drive inflation above 200% in 2023, Al Jazeera reported. The Senate had removed President Javier Milei's proposal to require a two-thirds vote to fire central bank governors. 4

In Guinea-Bissau, transitional president Maj. Gen. Horta Inta-a issued a decree moving presidential and legislative elections set for Dec. 6 to November 2028, citing a lack of 'technical, material and financial conditions.' The army seized power a year ago and had promised a one-year transition; the regional bloc ECOWAS had called for a short return to civilian rule. 5 6

Asia-Pacific

Official results in New Zealand leave Prime Minister Christopher Luxon's governing coalition one seat short of a majority in the 130-seat parliament. It now needs the centrist Opportunity Party's support, or the country may vote again; a Labour-led government is not possible even with that party's backing. 7

New Hampshire

A grocery warehouse strike drags on, and new research bears on how schools enforce the phone ban

With the Legislature not yet sworn in, New Hampshire's policy news this week came from a labor dispute and from education research. The research could shape how districts carry out the state's new school cellphone law. 1 2

Labor

About 255 Teamsters Local 633 members at Associated Grocers of New England's Pembroke headquarters and warehouse are in the fourth week of a strike that began Nov. 2, and the company has not agreed to bargain before Dec. 17, the union said. The main dispute is the company's demand to replace the workers' pension, which pays a set monthly benefit in retirement, with a 401(k) account, whose value depends on contributions and market returns. The company has kept some shipments moving with non-union staff and has called the union's messaging a 'misrepresentation of facts.' The cooperative supplies about 1,000 independent grocery stores across New England and New York. 1

Education

A working paper from researchers at Dartmouth, Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research, and the testing nonprofit NWEA found that schools requiring students to lock phones in pouches saw gains in math and reading scores. The study covered 3.3 million students in 4,600 schools from 2021 to 2026; gains began in the first year, continued for three more, and were similar across grade levels, race, gender, and prior achievement. The paper has not yet been peer-reviewed. 2

New Hampshire's 'bell to bell' law bans student phone use during class, between classes, and at lunch, but leaves enforcement to districts and does not require pouches; some, including Lebanon, use an 'off-and-away' rule instead. Because pouches cost money, the findings put a budget question in front of school boards. 2

Artificial Intelligence

States move to stop a bankrupt chatbot company from selling millions of private conversations

A bipartisan coalition of 42 state attorneys general is opposing Character.AI's plan, made during its Chapter 11 bankruptcy, to sell the chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to minors, The Verge reported. Chapter 11 lets a company reorganize or sell assets under a court's supervision. 1

The attorneys general say the proposed sale would not require the buyer to honor existing privacy policies, gives users no way to delete their data, and puts no limits on what the buyer could do with it, including reselling it. The first bidder was formed on Nov. 13 and lists no parent company or investors. The bankruptcy court will decide whether to approve the sale. New chatbot laws in New York, Connecticut, California, and Colorado take effect Jan. 1, 2027. 1
